What is this project?

Find Us is a research + engineering effort for an infrastructure-independent smartphone crowd navigation system. It is not "a BLE app" and not "indoor GPS": it is the whole stack that lets any phone — with no towers, no cloud and no account — join a temporary crowd, help relay an emergency signal, and be walked to it over a graph built from radios it can actually hear.

The one-line promise

  1. Every phone derives a per-incident team key from a plain text link — the whole crowd authenticates each other, server-free.
  2. Phones form a live topology graph (edges = radios actually heard), not a map of GPS.
  3. An emergency is a hop gradient that ripples outward; every device only holds hops it personally adopted.
  4. Every device can then walk you toward the lower hop using whatever it can physically measure — the navigation tier.

The honest map

This site keeps the research community’s rule: theoretical, implemented, and measured are never mixed. Every simulation on this page is a faithful teaching model of the actual engine (core/python → Kotlin → Swift), and every number is tagged:

  • MEASURED — produced by the reference implementation on this machine or the audit suite.
  • MODEL — a simplification for teaching; the real phone uses the engine, this is its inner workings.
  • FIELD OPEN — the physics we model but have not yet proven with phones on a real emergency floor.
  • NOT-RUN — a planned experiment that has not been executed. Nothing here pretends otherwise.

Why the whole stack matters

A person’s question — “which direction do I walk?” — is last in a chain. Before it can be answered honestly, you need: a way to authenticate without servers (the link & envelope MAC), a connection-free topology (advertisements, not sessions), a bound gradient relay (TTL, dedup, freshness), a rule that never fabricates a route (the one-hop back claims rule), a motion model (dead reckoning, barometrics), and finally a navigation tier negotiation so an old BLE4 phone and a new BLE5 phone still walk the same direction. Skip any one stage and the arrow you finally see is a guess.

Status in one breath

  • The reference engine and audits are complete and green: packet PDU (56-bit, FEC, anti-spoof MAC), a 91-check adversarial phone-to-phone robustness audit, and the 13-check compat/direction battery all pass.
  • A 50k-device envelope benchmark exists in simulation: single-frame processing ≈ 30 µs, MAC attach ≈ 14 µs, MAC verify ≈ 8 µs.
  • The app source (Android Kotlin + Compose, iOS SwiftUI) carries the engine, a packet layer, and CI that compiles both.
  • The manuscript (46 sections, ~34 printed pages) has been through a 5-seat academic review panel; the consensus fixes are incorporated.
  • What is still open: field validation of real BLE ranges, RSSI log-distance coefficients, rotate-to-find bearings, baro floor separation, and every [NOT-RUN] experiment this page will flag as you go.

The paper, unpacked

The full manuscript is 46 sections. This page is the explainer version — the ideas and the proof structure, with the honest gaps left visible. The raw artifacts are downloadable at the bottom.

1 · The problem — why this is hard

Indoor, mass-casualty, or disaster scenes have no working infrastructure. Prior art splits into two camps that never meet in one phone:location systems (need hardware: UWB anchors, AoA arrays, GPS, or a trained fingerprint map) and mesh messengers (send bytes, but never say which way to walk). This project asks for the whole thing on ordinary phones: a mesh, a route, and a voice that says “move toward that face” — using only measurables a commodity phone exposes.

2 · Prior-art lineage — what we built on

The design is a synthesis, not a novelty-sausage machine. Its typed fuses are drawn from: directed diffusion (interest gradients), DV-hop / APS (hop-count space), Doherty et al.’s location-free topology, and AFL (spring-embedding rigidity). The manuscript credits each lineage in its references. The honest contribution is the integration plus the honesty rules: an offline team-key identity layer, a connection-free topology, and a gradient that is structurally forbidden from fabricating a route.

3 · Architecture — the seven layers

1 Identity: install key + incident id → per-incident pseudonym. 2 Transport: BLE5 dual-role advertising/scanning, connection-free. 3 Packet: a 56-bit PDU with FEC and a rolling 16-bit envelope MAC. 4 Topology: edges are radios heard; stale nodes/edges expire. 5 Gradient: SOS relay with TTL, dedup window, freshness, and codified broadcast (Trickle + jitter). 6 Navigation tiers: from hop-gradient-only up to (future) UWB/Channel-Sounding. 7 Guidance honesty: a device only ever reports routes it can show, and only points along a direction it can physically justify.

4 · The one-hop honesty rule (the heart)

Any device at hop H > 0 only advertises hop H while it can still hear a live claim of hop H−1. If the fresher claim disappears, the device stops advertising — and the gradient collapses one ring at a time from the origin outward. This is what prevents “ghost gradients”: phantom routes that keep pointing people at a rescue that already ended. You can watch this happen live in the Gradient simulation by switching the origin off.

5 · Guidance and the conditional direction lemma

The guidance module proves (under a condition — §25.3 of the manuscript) that if a path of live H−1 claims exists to you, your next hop is on a path to the origin. The caveat is stated, not hidden: the lemma holds given the freshness condition and given neighbor claims are honest. The reviewer panel flagged exactly this class of assumption; the manuscript marks it provisional on the E31/E32 experiments which have not been run.

6 · Security without a server

Identity is per incident and pseudonymous. The link is the only secret distribution: anyone who scans it derives the same team key. Frames carry a rolling envelope MAC over (SOS_ID ‖ EPOCH ‖ PKT_TYPE) so a foreign frame is rejected before any graph write — the audit replays, forges and floods all day and the graph never moves. Privacy governance (§: "Identity & governance") explains what a pseudonym is and is not: a phone number of sorts that dies with the incident.

7 · What the reviewer panel did

The manuscript was passed through a 5-seat peer panel (Journal-fit + 3 reviews + Devil’s Advocate), verdict MAJOR REVISION. The consensus fixes are already in: placeholder numbers killed, every point estimate anchored to a run-id (exp_002_trickle, exp_009_async_trickle, exp_011_ar_gated_pdr, exp_012_terminal_handoff), qualitative findings marked with their method (e.g. [RES: qualitative]), and every executed vs reported step cross-checked in the paper’s Appendix J.1 audit.

8 · Ethics and retention

No account, no cloud, no behavioural tracking. An incident key can be retired by the incident creator; responders in the same incident share a capability-negotiated view. The manuscript discusses when retaining hops is a feature (disaster audit) vs a risk (civilian crowds) and does not pretend both can be simultaneously maximised.

9 · Research questions and honest answers

The paper is organised around research questions (RQ1–RQ12). Each has a status: answered in simulation (with run-id), qualitative, or provisional on a field experiment. RQ4’s adoption target and RQ11’s heading threshold were two places the panel found made-up-looking numbers; both are now explicit measured targets or marked provisional — nothing is silently rounded.

Mesh 101 — how a device enters the mesh

The exact thing people ask: “how does a third person join a working mesh?” It is six small steps, each honest. This page walks them with the real model: press step to advance the story of a new device walking into range of a live rescue.

The six steps

  1. Link. Someone gives you the incident link (a chat, a QR, a printed card). incident://…
  2. Key. Your phone derives the same team key everyone else derived from the same link. No server, no sync, no account.
  3. Scan. You begin dual-role: advertise your own identity, listen for others. Advertising itself is a packet you broadcast — even a joining phone is heard instantly.
  4. Authenticate. Every frame you hear must pass the rolling envelope MAC before it can touch your graph. A stranger’s frame is counted and dropped.
  5. Adopt. A neighbour’s live hop-3 claim is fresher than yours, so you adopt hop 4 (within TTL). You now hold one honest number.
  6. Back it. You may now advertise hop 4 — but only while that hop-3 claim stays live. The moment it dies, you fall silent. No ghosts.

Frequently shorthanded answers

“Is it like offline WhatsApp?” Partly: bytes hop phone-to-phone. But it also carries a gradient — numbers that say “I am 4 hops from the zero” — and a conscience that refuses to repeat a number it can no longer back.

“Does everyone need the same app?” Everyone in the incident needs a Find Us build. Cross-app participation is future work; the packet and key formats are published so a second implementer could interop.

“What if nobody’s nearby?” A lone phone carries nothing except its own heartbeats. The mesh is connection-free; presence is measured by hearing, and hearing needs a radio neighbour.

What a topology edge really means

An edge between two phones is not “we’re friends”. It is: you were heard recently on the advertising channel. The graph expires nodes after 120 s and edges after 60 s of silence, so the topology tracks physical reach, not a buddy list. A cold corridor suddenly separating two phones poisons exactly one edge.

Simulations — the engine, operated

Four teaching simulations built from the actual engine’s parameters (TTL 8, node expiry 120 s, edge 60 s, freshness 120 s, dedup window 20k, Trickle window ~1200 ms with jitter). These are the same models core/python runs; here you get the controls.

Gradient onboarding & the one-hop ghost rule

Watch rings of hop numbers radiate from the origin. Then enable origin silent and see the gradient collapse one ring at a time — each device stops advertising the moment its fresher claim dies. That is the ghost-kill in action. MODEL (faithful rules)

Trickle — bounded broadcast load

Each device advertises a short frame on a jittered slot, resting between slots. Even a 50k crowd is O(k) small frames/s per receiver, not O(N).

Strips are devices; bars are advert bursts, slots jittered so bursts rarely collide. The duty-cycle readout shows steady-state airtime per device. Reproduces the exp_002_trickle run family in miniature.

SOS flood, dedup, and rejections

Duplicates are absorbed by the dedup window; foreign frames are counted, MAC-rejected, and never touch the graph. Bars: accepted gradient updates vs rejected forge attempts.

Cached mule burst — carrying a gradient across a gap

A “mule” (a pedestrian, a runner with a phone, a drone) crosses a radio gap and bursts its cached gradient to the far side, letting a second mesh light up. Store-and-forward with the MULE_STORE_FORWARD flag is a first-class packet type in the PDU.

Glossary & reference numbers

Terms

Hop
The number of adopted radio steps from the emergency origin. Origin = hop 0.
Gradient
Per-device table: which neighbour claims which hop, and how fresh.
Envelope MAC
16-bit rolling authenticator over (SOS_ID ‖ EPOCH ‖ PKT_TYPE), keyed by the incident team key.
Trickle
Jittered, duty-cycled broadcast rhythm — small frames, often, predictably but not colliding.
Dedup window
Bound on packets re-entering the gradient state; 20,000-frame horizon in the engine.
Freshness / expiry
Nodes die at 120 s, edges at 60 s in the reference; a route claim is “live” for 120 s.
Nav tier
The lowest mutually measurable navigation signal two phones agree on.
Mule
A delay-tolerant carrier (runner/drone) that physically moves a cached gradient across a gap.
